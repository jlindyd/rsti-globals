Leading with Robustness™

Create value. Improve flow. Engineer dependable performance.

Leading with Robustness™ connects Operational Excellence, Lean Six Sigma, and Design for Six Sigma through one guiding question: Will the value we create hold up under real-world variation?

Operational Excellence aligns improvement with customer needs. Lean Six Sigma strengthens existing processes. Design for Six Sigma builds quality into new ones. Robustness Thinking™ brings them together by reducing the system's sensitivity to change.

02 — Principles

Lean removes the fat. Robustness builds the muscle.

Lean helps teams remove work that does not create customer value. Six Sigma helps them understand and reduce variation in process results. Robustness Thinking™ asks how to make a product, process, or organization less sensitive to the variation that remains. Together, they improve performance and help sustain it across different people, inputs, workloads, environments, and time.

01

Start with customer value

What outcome matters, and how will we know we delivered it?

Define quality, delivery, cost, safety, and reliability measures before choosing a tool.

02

Make waste visible

Which steps consume time or resources without improving the outcome?

Map the value flow and remove avoidable waiting, movement, rework, or handoffs.

03

Understand variation

Where and when do results differ, and what drives the difference?

Compare performance across shifts, material lots, equipment, demand levels, and operating conditions.

04

Reduce sensitivity

Which factors can't be fully controlled, and how can the system perform well despite them?

Change a design, operating window, control, or recovery path so a small input change doesn't cause a large performance loss.

05

Prevent mistakes and learn from operation

How will we detect problems early and feed what we learn back into improvement and design?

Combine mistake prevention, meaningful measures, frontline observations, and engineering feedback.

06

Verify performance over time

Does the result hold after the pilot and under real operating conditions?

Review capability, defects, flow, and reliability at agreed intervals, and adjust when conditions change.

Example · Reverse supply chain

Lean reveals repeated handling and waiting between assessment and disposition. Six Sigma shows that cycle time and decision accuracy vary by product type and shift. The team improves the workflow, then asks: will the new process still meet its targets when return volumes surge, product mix changes, or experienced staff are unavailable? Standard decision criteria, flexible capacity, clear escalation, and monitoring help preserve the gain. Removing steps creates efficiency; building capability keeps the process reliable under stress.

An improvement is complete when it creates value in practice and continues to perform as conditions change.

03 — The Framework

From customer value to reliable performance

Leading with Robustness™ connects strategy, improvement, and design in one learning cycle. We define the value that matters, study the current system for waste, variation, and gaps, then improve an existing process or design a new solution. We test how it responds to realistic change, and use operating evidence to strengthen it over time.

At every stage, ask: what changes?

  • Demand and product mix
  • Materials and supplier quality
  • Operators, skills, and shifts
  • Equipment and measurement systems
  • Environment and time

Which changes cause the greatest loss of value, and what can we change in the system to reduce that loss?
