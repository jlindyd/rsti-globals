Leading with Robustness™

Create value. Improve flow. Engineer dependable performance.

Leading with Robustness™ connects Operational Excellence, Lean Six Sigma, and Design for Six Sigma through one guiding question: Will the value we create hold up under real-world variation?

Operational Excellence aligns improvement with customer needs. Lean Six Sigma strengthens existing processes. Design for Six Sigma builds quality into new ones. Robustness Thinking™ brings them together by reducing the system's sensitivity to change.

02 — Principles

Lean removes the fat. Robustness builds the muscle.

Lean helps teams remove work that does not create customer value. Six Sigma helps them understand and reduce variation in process results. Robustness Thinking™ asks how to make a product, process, or organization less sensitive to the variation that remains. Together, they improve performance and help sustain it across different people, inputs, workloads, environments, and time.

01

Start with customer value

What outcome matters, and how will we know we delivered it?

Define quality, delivery, cost, safety, and reliability measures before choosing a tool.

02

Make waste visible

Which steps consume time or resources without improving the outcome?

Map the value flow and remove avoidable waiting, movement, rework, or handoffs.

03

Understand variation

Where and when do results differ, and what drives the difference?

Compare performance across shifts, material lots, equipment, demand levels, and operating conditions.

04

Reduce sensitivity

Which factors can't be fully controlled, and how can the system perform well despite them?

Change a design, operating window, control, or recovery path so a small input change doesn't cause a large performance loss.

05

Prevent mistakes and learn from operation

How will we detect problems early and feed what we learn back into improvement and design?

Combine mistake prevention, meaningful measures, frontline observations, and engineering feedback.

06

Verify performance over time

Does the result hold after the pilot and under real operating conditions?

Review capability, defects, flow, and reliability at agreed intervals, and adjust when conditions change.

Example · Reverse supply chain

Lean reveals repeated handling and waiting between assessment and disposition. Six Sigma shows that cycle time and decision accuracy vary by product type and shift. The team improves the workflow, then asks: will the new process still meet its targets when return volumes surge, product mix changes, or experienced staff are unavailable? Standard decision criteria, flexible capacity, clear escalation, and monitoring help preserve the gain. Removing steps creates efficiency; building capability keeps the process reliable under stress.

An improvement is complete when it creates value in practice and continues to perform as conditions change.

03 — The Framework

From customer value to reliable performance

Leading with Robustness™ connects strategy, improvement, and design in one learning cycle. We define the value that matters, study the current system for waste, variation, and gaps, then improve an existing process or design a new solution. We test how it responds to realistic change, and use operating evidence to strengthen it over time.

At every stage, ask: what changes?

  • Demand and product mix
  • Materials and supplier quality
  • Operators, skills, and shifts
  • Equipment and measurement systems
  • Environment and time

Which changes cause the greatest loss of value, and what can we change in the system to reduce that loss?

05 — The Pathway

A workshop that leads to action

The Leading with Robustness™ pathway turns learning into a focused 90-day pilot. Your team selects a meaningful challenge, establishes a trustworthy baseline, and chooses the right improvement or design route. We then test performance under the conditions the system will actually face and use the evidence to decide what to adopt, strengthen, or scale.

The workshop produces the charter, measures, route, and test plan.

The next 90 days produce evidence and a decision.

  1. Workshop · days 1–15

    Focus

    Operational Excellence

    Select one problem tied to customer value and a business priority. Name the sponsor, owner, team, scope, and desired result.

    Output: a one-page pilot charter.

  2. Days 15–30

    Establish the baseline

    Lean Six Sigma or DFSS

    Map the current value flow or define the new design's requirements. Check that measures and data are usable. Identify likely sources of variation.

    Output: a baseline and an initial variation and risk map.

  3. Days 30–45

    Choose the route

    Lean Six Sigma or DFSS

    For an existing process, investigate causes and select a Lean Six Sigma improvement pilot. For a new or redesigned solution, translate customer needs into a DFSS design pilot.

    Output: an agreed pilot design, test plan, resources, and decision criteria.

  4. Days 45–75

    Pilot and stress-test

    Lean Six Sigma or DFSS

    Run the improvement or evaluate the design. Test the conditions most likely to affect performance: volume, inputs, operators, equipment, environment.

    Output: results against the baseline, including under relevant variation.

  5. Days 75–90

    Decide and sustain

    Operational Excellence

    Review the evidence with the sponsor. Adopt, adjust, expand, or stop. Assign controls, monitoring, and follow-up.

    Output: a decision record, sustainment plan, and next-stage roadmap.

Lean Six Sigma route · improve what already exists

A team targets return-processing delays. It maps the current flow, measures cycle time and rework, tests the main causes, and pilots a revised process. Its robustness test: does the gain hold across shifts, product types, and demand peaks?

DFSS route · design for future requirements

A team develops a new inspection process. It defines customer and operational requirements, compares concepts, and tests how sensing accuracy and throughput respond to changes in parts, lighting, equipment, and workload before deployment.

06 — Where It Applies

A different kind of variation in every setting

Engineering

Design for real conditions

Translate customer needs into requirements, test sensitivity to variation, and use operating evidence to strengthen lifecycle reliability.

The challenge

A design meets its target in a controlled test but performs inconsistently across environments, users, or component tolerances.

What we examine

Performance spread, failure rate, reliability over time, recovery behavior, and customer impact.

Manufacturing

Sustain quality and flow

Remove waste, reduce process variation, and build operations that maintain performance across shifts, inputs, equipment conditions, and demand levels.

The challenge

A process meets its target on one line or shift but produces rework, downtime, or inconsistent output elsewhere.

What we examine

First-pass yield, cycle time, rework, downtime, process capability, and cost of poor quality.

Supply chain

Make complex flows more resilient

Reveal delays and rework, improve decisions, and test whether gains hold when volumes, product mix, and capacity change.

The challenge

Returns, repairs, or fulfillment decisions slow down as product mix, volume, or available capacity changes.

What we examine

Turnaround time, throughput, decision accuracy, labor productivity, inventory, and return on investment.

Featured example · Reverse supply chain

Value Stream Mapping identified delays and non-value-added activity. Lean Six Sigma guided process improvement. Digital Twin and AI Agent work supported assessment and decisions, and performance was evaluated against operational and business measures.

Whether the work begins in design, on the factory floor, or across a supply chain, the question is the same: how do we create value that continues to hold under changing conditions?

Workshop example · 2 days · University of Houston

Operational Excellence by Design

Leveraging Design Thinking and Design for Six Sigma to compress time.

To see the process in action, participants work through a hands-on case study in five timed steps: creating and optimizing a paper helicopter.

  1. 1
    Empathize 10 minDiscuss the customer complaints as a group, then drop the provided helicopter from different heights and angles and note the inconsistencies.
  2. 2
    Define 10 minFrame the problem: how might we design a helicopter that spins consistently, however the angle it is held? Write a clear problem statement.
  3. 3
    Ideate 15 minBrainstorm solutions. Think about shape, size, balance, and weight distribution, and sketch designs to test.
  4. 4
    Prototype 20 minCreate at least two prototypes with scissors and tape, balancing weight and adjusting blade size or shape. Document each change.
  5. 5
    Test 20 minDrop each prototype from a fixed height. Record spin, stability, and sensitivity to drop angle, time the fall, then compare results as a group.

Design Thinking brings a user-focused, creative, iterative way to find the right problem and ideas.

DFSS adds a systematic way to make the design reliable and meet quality and customer expectations.

For leaders, the combination means competitive advantage and operational efficiency, with solutions that stay innovative and responsive to changing demands.

Method overview

Design for Robustness with DFSS

Build reliability and quality in before launch, rather than fixing it after.

Design for Six Sigma is a systematic way to design or redesign processes, products, and services for high performance, reliability, and customer satisfaction. It is applied in the design phase, so the result meets customer expectations from the start.

IDDOV, not DMAIC. Where Lean Six Sigma improves what already exists (DMAIC), DFSS follows IDDOV: Identify, Define, Develop, Optimize, Verify.

Robust Engineering in three passes: concept design, parameter design, and tolerance design, so performance holds up under variation.

Robustness changes the question from "does it hit the target?" to "how much does performance change when conditions change?" This follows the Taguchi loss function rather than the traditional goalpost mentality, and it is practised through hands-on robustness experiments.
